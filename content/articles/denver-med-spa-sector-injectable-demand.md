The number of med-spas operating across the Denver metro area has grown noticeably over the past two years, driven largely by rising demand for injectable treatments like neuromodulators and dermal fillers.

Industry association data reviewed by local business groups shows the pace of new med-spa openings in the metro area accelerating well beyond growth in traditional day spas, a shift that has prompted state regulators to increase scrutiny of who is legally permitted to administer injectable treatments. Colorado requires injectables to be administered or directly supervised by a licensed medical professional, a rule that has become a focal point as new locations open rapidly.

## Regulatory Scrutiny Increases

State health officials have flagged a rise in complaints related to improperly supervised injectable treatments, prompting renewed enforcement attention on med-spas operating without adequate medical oversight. Industry groups representing licensed providers say the growth has made consistent enforcement more difficult, since the sheer number of new locations has outpaced regulatory inspection capacity.

Dr. Naomi Castellanos, a Denver-based physician who oversees injectable services at a Cherry Creek med-spa, says the regulatory attention is overdue. "This industry grew faster than oversight could keep up," she said. "Patients assume medical-grade oversight just because it's called a med-spa, and that's not always accurate."

## Consumers Navigating a Crowded Market

For consumers, the rapid growth has made it harder to distinguish between medically supervised practices and lower-oversight operations offering similar treatments at lower price points. Consumer advocacy groups have started publishing guidance encouraging patients to verify the credentials of whoever will actually be administering an injection, rather than assuming a spa's marketing reflects its staffing.

Industry groups expect the sector's growth to continue, driven by both rising consumer demand and lower barriers to opening a med-spa compared to a traditional medical practice, though several are pushing state lawmakers to consider updated licensing requirements specific to injectable treatments.
