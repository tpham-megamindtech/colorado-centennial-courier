Small discrepancies can do outsized damage. A client rarely walks away over a single dollar, but they will walk away over the feeling that the amount they were told and the amount they were charged didn't match. This report on Elite Nail and Spa is a clear, low-drama example of exactly that, and a reminder that pricing accuracy is a trust issue as much as a money one.

## A Simple, Understood Visit

The visit itself was straightforward and handled reasonably. The client had recently had surgery on one foot, so she was really only having one foot done, with no massage or soak. She understood and accepted that limitation herself, and simply wanted the nails painted. There was no complaint about the service or the accommodation, which makes what happened at checkout the sole sticking point.

## Quoted One Price, Charged Another

At checkout the client was told the total was $35. She paid with her debit card and added a $3 tip, noting she'd usually leave more but felt $3 was fair for a one-foot service. Later, checking her account, she saw a charge of $36.25, an amount that didn't line up with the $35 she'd been quoted. As she put it, she doesn't understand why a salon would tell someone one price and then charge their card a different one. That mismatch, small as it is, is what decided her not to return.

## Editorial Perspective

The dollar figure here is tiny, but the principle behind it is not. When a client is quoted $35 and their statement later reads $36.25, they're left to wonder where the difference came from, and whether they can trust any number the salon gives them. Often the culprit is an undisclosed card-processing fee or a rounding practice that made sense internally but was never explained. Either way, the fix is transparency: the amount charged should match the amount quoted, and if a card fee applies, the client should hear about it before they pay, not discover it on their bank statement. A surprise of a dollar and a quarter reads as carelessness at best and something sneaky at worst, and neither is worth losing a customer over.

## Key Takeaways

- Charge exactly what you quote. The number on the card should match the total the client was told at checkout, down to the cent.

- Disclose any card fee up front. If paying by card adds a surcharge, say so before running the payment, so it's a choice and never a surprise.

- Offer an itemized receipt. A clear breakdown of service, fees, and tip removes any doubt about where the total came from.

- Treat small discrepancies as seriously as big ones. A tiny unexplained gap can feel sneakier than a large obvious charge, and it erodes trust just as fast.

## Final Thoughts

Everything about this visit went fine until the numbers stopped matching. The client was understanding about her limited service and even thoughtful about the tip, so losing her over a $1.25 discrepancy is a genuinely avoidable outcome. Quoting one clear price, charging exactly that, and explaining any fee before payment would have kept a fair-minded customer coming back.
